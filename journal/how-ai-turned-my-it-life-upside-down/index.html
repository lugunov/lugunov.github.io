How AI turned my comfortably boring IT life upside down

After more than twenty years in IT, generative AI made building software feel new again.

For more than twenty years, IT has been my profession.

And at some point, as usually happens with professions, it became… well, a profession.

Interesting projects, complicated systems, architecture, integrations, meetings, PowerPoints, Jira, Confluence — all the normal stuff. I still liked technology, but that feeling I had as a teenager when I could spend an entire evening programming something completely useless just because it was fun had mostly disappeared.

Then GenAI arrived.

Looking back, I think this is only the third technology shift in my life that genuinely changed my relationship with computers.

The first was discovering programming at school.

The second was the Internet.

And now this.

It started very innocently

Like most people, I started with the free version of ChatGPT.

Mostly for fun.

I asked random questions, tested what it knew and tried to catch it saying something stupid.

Then I discovered a genuinely practical use case: fixing my English emails.

English is not my native language, and while after many years I can obviously communicate reasonably well, my written English can occasionally become a little… creative.

ChatGPT fixed that nicely.

Then I started asking everyday questions. Comparing things. Researching stuff. Asking it to explain something instead of opening ten browser tabs.

And suddenly I was using it every day.

Then I started hitting the free limits.

So I subscribed.

Not because I had some brilliant AI strategy.

I simply wanted to keep talking to it.

Then things became more technical

Gradually our conversations moved into familiar territory.

Software architecture.

Systems.

Integration.

Technical ideas.

And eventually code.

I had always had ideas for little software products. Nothing world-changing — usually things that solved some problem I personally had.

But there was one problem.

After spending eight hours working in IT, opening an IDE in the evening and starting another software project was about as attractive as doing another work shift for free.

There is probably some truth in the saying that once your hobby becomes your profession, you lose the hobby.

Then I installed Codex.

And initially had absolutely no clue what I was doing.

My prompts were primitive. Results were sometimes weird. Things broke.

I watched a few videos, read some examples, tried again.

Prompt by prompt, something started to click.

And then the dangerous moment arrived:

it actually started working.

That was it.

I was hooked.

Suddenly I was building again

The workflow became strangely natural.

I would discuss an idea with ChatGPT, challenge it, structure it, turn it into something concrete and then take the implementation into Codex.

Something wouldn't work.

We would fix it.

Something would look terrible.

We would redesign it.

One prompt became ten. Ten became fifty.

And somewhere along the way an experiment became a project.

Then another project appeared.

Then another.

The funny thing is that it reminded me of learning programming when I was young.

Try something.

Break it.

Figure out why.

Fix it.

Immediately think of something else to try.

Except now I had an extremely patient coding companion who never got tired of my questions.

And then one of those experiments escaped into the real world

One of my old ideas eventually became Looply, a mobile app.

I had never written an iPhone app before.

I wasn't a Flutter developer.

I knew practically nothing about TestFlight, App Store Connect or Apple's publishing process.

A year earlier, that combination alone would probably have been enough for me to say:

“Nice idea. Maybe one day.”

Instead, we built it.

There were plenty of iterations, wrong turns, redesigns and bugs.

At some point even the name changed.

The first prototype was WhenDo. Eventually it became Looply.

And then, after an unexpectedly painful adventure with Apple — during which I occasionally felt that writing the app was easier than convincing Apple to let me publish it — it was approved.

My first real public software product was in the App Store.

Will millions of people download it?

Probably not.

Do I care?

Not that much.

The important part for me was that something that had lived only in my head became a real product that anyone could actually install.

That felt surprisingly good.

And then things got slightly out of control

Looply wasn't the end.

It was more like proof that the whole idea worked.

Now I have several pet projects running in parallel.

Personal finance.

Crypto tracking.

AI assistants.

Automation.

Various small tools built around problems that annoy me personally.

Some of them may become useful products.

Some will probably die quietly somewhere in GitHub.

That's fine.

Because the important thing is that I'm building again.

For fun.

After more than twenty years in professional IT, that feels surprisingly refreshing.

My third IT revolution

That's why I think GenAI is my third big IT shift.

Programming showed me that I could tell computers what to do.

The Internet connected everything.

And GenAI is changing what I personally can build.

It hasn't magically made me a professional mobile developer, UX designer, frontend engineer and database expert.

But it has dramatically lowered the barrier between:

“I have an idea.”

and

“Let's see if I can actually build it.”

My existing experience still matters.

Architecture matters.

Understanding systems matters.

Knowing how to break problems down matters.

Judgement definitely matters — especially when your AI companion confidently produces complete nonsense.

But now I have leverage I simply didn't have before.

Somewhere along the way I apparently became an AI-assisted hobby programmer.

And these days I once again catch myself spending evenings building things simply because I'm curious whether they'll work.

Occasionally until I hit the token limit.

Which, I suppose, is the modern equivalent of your parents telling you to switch the computer off and go to bed.

IT feels new again.

And I really didn't expect that.
