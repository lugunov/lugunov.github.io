Are We Architecting Enterprises — or Just Integrating Our Own Mistakes?

How good technology decisions can create a surprisingly complicated enterprise landscape.

“Simplicity is the ultimate sophistication.”

It's a principle I've come back to throughout my career. To me, good architecture is about finding a clear way through a complex problem — not adding more complexity just because we can.

And yet, after more than twenty years in enterprise technology, I keep coming back to the same question:

How do we end up with so many systems doing pieces of the same thing?

One process, many systems

Take logistics. Moving goods from A to B can be complicated in practice. There are plenty of steps, decisions and things that can go wrong.

But from a business perspective, it's still one continuous process.

Separating finance from logistics makes sense to me. They are different domains with different responsibilities. But why do we so often split the logistics chain itself across a growing collection of applications?

One system handles this stage. Another handles that one. Then another arrives because it has a particularly good feature.

Each decision can make perfect sense on its own.

Put them all together, though, and we may end up with overlapping capabilities, duplicated data and a lot of integrations to build and maintain.

Then we invest in analytics and data harmonisation so we can see the end-to-end process again — the same process we divided across all those systems in the first place.

And now there is AI. If the data is fragmented and nobody is quite sure who owns what, making intelligent decisions from it becomes rather more difficult.

It makes me wonder how much of our IT investment is solving business problems, and how much is helping us manage complexity we created ourselves.

A solar system, not a zoo

The way I like to think about it is as a solar system.

There is a sun in the middle: a coherent business backbone that supports as much of the core process as makes sense, with clear ownership of the important data.

Around it are specialised satellites — systems that handle capabilities which genuinely belong outside that core.

I'm not suggesting that everything should go into one enormous monolith, or that an organisation should depend on a single vendor for absolutely everything.

The point is to have a deliberate centre of gravity, clear boundaries and fewer unnecessary overlaps.

Sometimes, before asking “How do we integrate another system?”, it is worth asking a slightly less exciting question:

Do we need another system at all?

Who gets to ask the question?

That brings me to the part that is less about technology and more about decisions.

Who actually shapes the long-term landscape? CIOs and CTOs? Business leaders? Enterprise architects? External advisers?

And when a strategic platform is being chosen, or a build-versus-buy decision is on the table, whose voice is heard?

I understand why individual decisions are often made under pressure. There are budgets, deadlines, urgent business needs and transformation targets. Waiting for a perfect answer is rarely an option.

But a series of sensible local decisions can still leave us with an enterprise architecture that doesn't make much sense as a whole.

That's why I think architecture needs a seat at the table before the technology is selected — not only afterwards, when someone needs to connect everything together.

Our role isn't just to make systems work together. It's also to help leadership decide which systems should exist in the first place.

Building a complex system is surprisingly easy.

Designing a simple one is where the real sophistication lies.
