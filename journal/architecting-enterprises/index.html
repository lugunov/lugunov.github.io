Are We Architecting Enterprises — or Just Integrating Our Own Mistakes?

How good technology decisions can create bad enterprise architecture.

“Simplicity is the ultimate sophistication” is a principle I have advocated throughout my career. Great architecture should solve complex business problems elegantly. Yet after more than two decades in enterprise technology, I keep seeing the opposite.

One business process, a software zoo

Take logistics, an industry close to my heart. For centuries, its fundamental objective has remained the same: move something from A to B. Modern logistics involves enormous operational complexity, of course, but at its heart it is still a continuous business process.

Now look at the technology landscapes we build to support it.

Separating financial systems from logistics platforms makes perfect sense. These are different business domains with different responsibilities. But within logistics itself, I often see something quite different.

Instead of using one capable platform to support the transportation lifecycle end to end, we introduce multiple specialised systems, each covering a small part of the same process. One application handles one stage, another takes over the next, and yet another fills a gap somewhere in between.

Soon we have overlapping capabilities, duplicated data, custom integrations and additional layers just to make the chain work together. What could have been a coherent process becomes a collection of technological silos.

Then we invest even more in analytics, data harmonisation and integration to reconstruct the end-to-end picture we fragmented ourselves. As AI becomes part of the picture, fragmented data and unclear ownership become even bigger challenges.

Sometimes I wonder how much of our IT investment goes into solving business problems, and how much goes into managing the complexity we created ourselves.

A solar system, not a zoo

What if we approached enterprise architecture differently? Imagine a solar system.

At its centre is the Sun: a strong, coherent business backbone covering as much of the core end-to-end process as reasonably possible. It is where essential business transactions happen and critical data has clear ownership.

Around it orbit specialised applications — planets and satellites — providing capabilities that genuinely belong outside the core.

Not everything needs to be in the Sun. I am certainly not advocating one giant monolith or dependence on a single vendor. The idea is a deliberate centre of gravity, clear boundaries, fewer unnecessary overlaps and integrations designed for a purpose.

Before asking “How do we integrate another system?”, it is worth asking: Do we need another system at all?

Who makes the call?

Who shapes the long-term technology landscape? CIOs, CTOs, business leaders, enterprise architects, external advisers? Whose voice matters when choosing strategic platforms or deciding whether to build or buy?

Urgency, budgets and transformation targets naturally influence decisions. But a series of sensible local decisions can produce an unreasonable enterprise architecture.

Architecture needs a seat at the table before technology is selected, not only afterwards when someone must connect it all. Our role is not merely to make systems work together. It is to help leadership decide which systems should exist in the first place.

Building a complex system is surprisingly easy. Designing a simple one is where the real sophistication lies.
