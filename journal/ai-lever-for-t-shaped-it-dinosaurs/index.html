AI Is a Lever for T-Shaped IT Dinosaurs

Generative AI does not replace experience. It gives experienced people more leverage to turn knowledge into execution.

I got seriously hooked on programming in the mid-1990s in Ukraine, in the era of ZX Spectrum, 286s, 386s and the first Pentiums. Back then, every new generation of hardware felt like a small revolution, and learning usually meant trial and error, copied software, limited documentation and a lot of curiosity.

It was a very different IT world. Software was difficult to get, documentation was limited, Internet access was barely available to most of us, and a lot of what circulated around was unofficial, copied or simply passed from one person to another.

So we learned mostly by trial and error. You tried something, broke it, stared at it for a while, changed something else and checked what happened. There were no YouTube tutorials, Stack Overflow answers or AI assistants patiently waiting for your next stupid question.

And somehow, that made it incredibly interesting.

I had already discovered programming at school, but during those years it became a real obsession. Then, as often happens, the hobby became a profession, and once your hobby becomes your job, something changes.

From programming to enterprise IT

Over the years I moved deeper and deeper into enterprise IT: SAP, integration, architecture, platforms, networks, databases, APIs, events, cloud, security and business processes.

My scope kept getting broader, but at the same time I never lost curiosity about everything happening outside my immediate professional bubble.

Python looked interesting. Java looked interesting. Web development looked interesting. Mobile development looked interesting. Cloud-native technologies, data engineering, new frameworks — basically, I wanted to understand everything.

There was only one small problem: there was never enough time.

You can become deep in one or two areas and stay reasonably informed about many others, but you cannot properly learn every language, framework, platform and tool that appears around you. At least I couldn't.

Over time I became what is usually called a T-shaped professional: deep expertise in some areas, especially enterprise architecture and SAP-related technologies, combined with a broad understanding of the wider IT landscape.

That worked very well professionally. But whenever I wanted to actually build something outside my main area, the same barrier appeared.

I usually understood roughly what needed to happen. I understood architectures, APIs, data, integration, networking, databases and infrastructure.

But knowing what should exist and being able to personally implement it are two very different things.

Then GenAI changed the equation

This is where AI became a lever for me.

Not a replacement for experience. A lever.

Suddenly, all that horizontal knowledge accumulated over decades became much more actionable.

I could discuss an architecture with ChatGPT, move from an idea into implementation, use Codex to build something in a technology I had never professionally worked with, inspect the result, challenge it, change direction and keep going.

I still needed to understand what I was looking at. In fact, I think that part became even more important, because AI can generate code very quickly — and it can generate nonsense very quickly too.

The advantage of experience is that you often recognise the difference.

Maybe not at the level of every line of syntax, but at the level that matters: Does this architecture make sense? Where will it break? What is unnecessarily complicated? What happens to the data? Where are the security boundaries? Is this maintainable? Do I really need another service here?

Those questions do not disappear just because AI can write code.

From “one day” to actually building

For years I had wanted to build my own mobile application.

When smartphones appeared, I always had this idea somewhere in the back of my mind: one day I should write something for iPhone and Android.

“One day”, of course, is a very convenient place to store projects that will probably never happen.

Learning mobile development properly was always competing with work, family, other interests and another ten technologies I also wanted to learn.

Then AI removed enough friction that “one day” gradually became “let's try it tonight”.

That eventually became Looply: Predictive Reminders, my first real application released publicly in the App Store.

The idea itself was not revolutionary. I had a personal problem I wanted to solve, understood roughly what the product should do, and used ChatGPT and Codex to bridge the implementation gap.

There were many iterations, wrong turns, redesigns and things that simply did not work. But eventually something that had lived in my head for years became a real product that anyone could download.

I am still not a professional mobile developer, and that actually makes the point stronger.

AI did not magically inject years of Flutter experience into my brain. It simply gave me enough leverage to turn my existing knowledge into something I previously would not have built.

Then came Financial OS

Another example is something I call my Financial OS.

I have been tracking our family finances for more than twenty years.

It started, of course, in Excel.

In the beginning, the spreadsheet was relatively simple. Over the years it accumulated more and more layers — categories, calculations, reports, history, different views and all the little things that made sense specifically for the way I wanted to manage our finances.

But underneath all those layers, it was still Excel.

And for probably most of those twenty years, I had the same thought somewhere in the back of my mind:

One day I should turn this into a proper application.

I tried looking at existing personal finance products, but none of them really matched the way I wanted to work. They were solving someone else's version of the problem.

I had my own requirements, my own logic and, after twenty years, quite a few opinions about how my financial system should work.

So the spreadsheet kept growing.

Then GenAI arrived.

And within a couple of months, I had built something surprisingly close to what I had been imagining for twenty years.

That still feels slightly ridiculous when I say it out loud.

Something I had postponed for two decades because it would require too much time, too many technologies and too much learning suddenly became a realistic pet project.

Maybe one day I will show Financial OS to the world. Maybe I will not.

Because I also realise that there are probably thousands of projects like this. Personal finance is deeply personal, and everyone seems to have their own idea of the “right” way to track money.

In a way, that is exactly the point.

GenAI makes it possible to build software not only for a market, but for your own very specific way of doing things.

And sometimes that is enough.

AI as a professional lever

The same thing is starting to happen in my daily work.

Architecture work contains a surprising amount of routine: gathering information, doing impact assessments, checking requirements against architecture principles, preparing review material, maintaining governance records, connecting decisions across Jira and Confluence, and following up on things that humans are notoriously bad at remembering consistently.

A lot of this still requires architectural judgement. But not every step requires an architect manually moving information between documents or repeatedly checking the same rules.

This is where I see another major lever.

AI can take over more of the mechanical work and allow an architect to spend more time on the decisions that actually require experience.

And this is what made me start thinking that one of the most interesting groups to benefit from GenAI may be people like me.

The T-shaped IT dinosaurs.

The T-shaped dinosaur advantage

There is an interesting advantage in being an IT dinosaur.

After enough years in technology, you start seeing patterns repeat.

Languages change. Frameworks change. Vendors change.

The fundamentals change much more slowly.

Data still needs to live somewhere. Systems still need boundaries. Distributed systems still fail. Networks still have latency. Security shortcuts still come back to haunt you. Integration still becomes complicated surprisingly quickly.

And business requirements still somehow manage to contradict each other.

A broad understanding of those fundamentals, combined with deep expertise in some areas, gives AI something extremely valuable to amplify.

That is why I increasingly see GenAI as a force multiplier for T-shaped people.

The deep vertical part gives you judgement and context. The broad horizontal part allows you to move across domains. AI then reduces the cost of turning that knowledge into execution.

Before GenAI, I might understand Python, web development or mobile architecture conceptually but still not have enough time to become productive in all of them.

Now I do not necessarily need to become an expert in every technology before I can use it meaningfully.

That is a very different equation.

I can feel myself accelerating

This is probably the part I enjoy most.

I can actually feel my own pace increasing.

An idea that previously might have stayed in a notebook can become a prototype. A technology I would previously have put on a six-month learning list can become something I start experimenting with tonight. A repetitive architecture process can become a candidate for automation.

One project creates an idea for another, and the better I become at working with these tools, the more opportunities I start noticing around me.

I am not becoming younger. Unfortunately, AI has not solved that problem yet.

But technologically, I currently feel almost the opposite of becoming obsolete.

I feel that decades of accumulated IT knowledge have suddenly become more useful, because I now have a lever that allows me to apply that knowledge much more broadly and much faster.

Maybe being an IT dinosaur is not such a bad position after all.

Especially when someone finally gives the dinosaur power tools. :)
